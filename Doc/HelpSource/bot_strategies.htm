TRP Bot Strategies

These summaries describe the bundled TRP players. Many are community contributions; the author and source header are retained in each script. Strategies are heuristics, not guarantees, and actual behavior may differ from these summaries. The script source is the definitive reference.

Each filename in parentheses identifies the bot being described, not a source from which another bot copied code. Strategy inspiration credits an idea; code adaptation or code hybrid identifies modified or combined routines from other bots. Original author credits are retained; later repository variants are identified separately. Statements about a bot being strong, weak, or best suited to particular games are historical author assessments, not guarantees or current tournament rankings.

Cooperation and program identity

Cooperation is implemented by individual scripts, not by a game-wide alliance setting. These scripts compare PProgram, the script filename, not the player's display name. Copies using the same filename recognize each other; differently named scripts do not become allies merely because they share code. In particular, Walker2 and Walker3 are not allies, and Frank and Frank+ do not form a cross-version alliance.

Borg, Walker2 and Walker3 have built-in same-program cooperation, without an on/off flag. Their normal play favors the coalition against outsiders, but cooperation does not guarantee permanent nonaggression: endgame behavior can allow attacks between copies. Walker2 and Walker3 retain a special territory-23 coalition endgame attack convention.

Digger, Defender, Hunter, Pitbull and Slave have mild same-program favoritism, not a nonaggression pact. Their attack evaluation multiplies the force-ratio score by 0.90 when the target belongs to another copy of the same program. This makes that target less attractive but still allows an attack. There is no cooperation toggle for this preference. Slave's separate master-following behavior can support a player using any program; it does not require another Slave.

Frank and Frank+ each declare a source-code Boolean Cooperative, set to False in the standard scripts and stored in buffer 29 during initialization. Changing it to True changes selected floating-bubble and continent-breaking attack branches, including Australia and North America. It is not a blanket alliance switch: some same-program continent protection exists even with the flag off, and some flag-controlled branches also change attacks against outsiders. This is a script setting, not a checkbox in the game interface.

Strategies

  • Actuary (actuary.trp) evaluates attacks with exact dice probabilities, abandons battles whose expected value turns unfavorable, plans eliminations, and concentrates forces toward a defensible continent.
  • Alexander (alexander.trp) assigns and attacks continents; its source recommends games with one or two other computer players.
  • Australian (australian.trp) is a code adaptation of Descartes that travels widely while eventually aiming to return to Australia.
  • Australian+ (australian+.trp) modifies Australian with Vexer's player-elimination code, as credited by its author. Unlike Doomstack's strategy inspiration, this is documented code reuse. It targets vulnerable human opponents and helps finish winning games.
  • Australian09 (australian09.trp) is a 2009-10 rewrite with a different strategy from Australian; it is designed for games with many players and combination-based card trading.
  • Balancer (balancer.trp) splits reinforcements between offense and defense, strengthening favorable attack fronts and reinforcing threatened borders.
  • Basic (Basic.trp) focuses on gaining territories rather than completing continents.
  • BonusSaboteur (bonussaboteur.trp) takes a cheap territory from an opponent to break its continent bonus; otherwise, it targets a weak border.
  • Borg (borg.trp) cooperates with other players using the same program; see cooperation and program identity for recognition and endgame limitations.
  • Brainless V (brainless V.trp) is a repository restoration of Chiel ten Brinke's player, with safety guards around its near-original ConquerArea elimination planner. That planner is incomplete and diagnostic: it does not select attack targets. Normal attack selection still operates independently of it. This is not an untouched original release.
  • Brainless VI (brainless vi.trp) is a later repository variant derived from Chiel ten Brinke's Brainless V. It uses a bounded, weighted route search to choose the first step toward a reachable weak opponent and replans after each attack. The route planner is a later modification, not a release by the original author.
  • Brainless IV (brainless_iv.trp) is a persistent, power-seeking player intended for ten-player games with progression-based card trading; its author notes it is less effective under other rules.
  • CardCollector (cardcollector.trp) seeks one low-risk conquest each turn to collect a card, then stops attacking for that turn. It favors weak adjacent territories and avoids costly campaigns.
  • Vulture (vulture.trp) collects one cheap card from adjacent territories with at most two defenders and at least 3:1 available army superiority. It also hunts opponents with at least four cards and at most four territories when a continuous route of at most eight captures fits its conservative army budget and guaranteed progressive card reward exceeds its estimated cost. It replans after each roll, counts intervening enemies, and never budgets captured cards before the elimination. It ignores continents and does not infer third-party damage from weak defenders. Adapted with AI assistance from two Gemini-generated proposals.
  • Tortoise (tortoise.trp) holds an owned chokepoint for twelve personal turns, attacking only to recover previously held territory in its defended continent. Afterwards it balances exposed borders against adjacent enemy armies and advances its wall by one conquest per turn when the wall has more than thirty armies. It leaves two armies behind where occupation rules permit, consolidates interior troops one adjacent step at a time along owned paths, and has no early card-taking or combat-odds exception. It selects a region heuristically, without a fixed Australia preference. Adapted with AI assistance from a Gemini-generated proposal.
  • Kamikaze (kamikaze.trp) concentrates reinforcements on its strongest front, selects the weakest enemy bordering any stack above three armies, and commits until conquest or exhaustion. It stops after one conquest and moves all available occupation armies forward. It ignores continents, combat odds and elimination payback; interior armies are consolidated toward a reachable front. Adapted with AI assistance from a Gemini-generated proposal, it is a high-risk CardCollector-like variant, not a proven competitive strategy.
  • Chokepoint (chokepoint.trp) values strategically connected territories and continent entry points. It seeks control of routes between regions when its local force ratio is favorable.
  • Comeback (comeback.trp) targets weak opponents and low-risk territories when behind. As its position improves, it raises its attack threshold and invests in continent bonuses.
  • Constant (constant.trp), originally named Constantin, was written by Konstantin Schwarze and is based on Simple. Its archived TurboRisk 1.2 source describes it as a "black box," but its code reveals a continent-focused strategy: placement weighs continent bonuses against border counts and relative forces, reinforces vulnerable borders of continents it owns, and places armies on the pressured front best weighted by those priorities. Attacks favor valuable continents and reduced-front routes, subject to a weighted force-ratio threshold. After conquest it balances armies between two fronts or moves forces forward from an interior territory; fortification sends the largest eligible interior reserve to a neighboring front.
  • Crusader (crusader.trp) follows a strict set of rules without adapting its decisions.
  • Defender (defender.trp) is a Digger offshoot that uses continent bonuses to defend border territories and becomes more defensive as it conquers. It uses focused "do or die" attacks and mild same-program attack favoritism.
  • Descartes (descartes.trp) builds forces inconspicuously before launching major attacks; its source describes it as designed for games with nine other computer players. In the archived page, its author called it a "stealth specialist" that could get into trouble if another player became strong too quickly.
  • Descartes09 (descartes09.trp) is a 2009-10 rewrite of Descartes with a different strategy, designed for games with many players and combination-based card trading.
  • Digger (digger.trp) tries to conquer at least one territory per turn and is less focused on taking and holding continents than Napoleon. It includes focused "do or die" attacks. Its author noted that it was formerly named unsimple.trp. It has mild same-program attack favoritism.
  • Challenger (challenger.trp) is the historical pre-stalemate Doomstack strategy: it builds a concentrated army, takes inexpensive territories for cards, plans eliminations, and pursues continents in smaller games. It does not include the current Doomstack's giant-stack stalemate breaker. Like Doomstack, it credits Brainless IV and Vexer for strategy ideas, not copied source code.
  • Doomstack (doomstack.trp) builds a concentrated army, takes inexpensive territories for cards, and saves strength for player eliminations or a decisive sweep. It also plans elimination routes, pursues continents in smaller games, and attacks giant stacks to break stalemates. Brainless IV (Mattias Beimers) inspired the concentrated-stack approach; Vexer inspired estimating elimination-route costs to capture cards. These are strategy credits, not source-code reuse. Doomstack's simpler greedy route search was independently implemented.
  • Endeavour09 (endeavour09.trp) starts with an open Endeavour-style strategy and switches to Australian09 after taking a continent. It tries to avoid rivals' likely starting continents.
  • Era$or-bot (era$or-bot.trp) adapts to the number and type of opponents, seeks continents, and works to protect them. Its author described it as their first bot, based on Simple, and reported that it won often in tests against the bots then included with TurboRisk.
  • Finisher (finisher.trp) is an aggressive end-game strategy intended to be combined with another TRP; it takes over when its army exceeds the combined opposing armies and attempts to finish quickly.
  • Fluid (Fluid.trp) tries to reduce the number of fronts it must defend.
  • Frank (frank.trp) is a Frankenstein-style combination of attack, placement, occupation, and fortification routines from several players, organized around a large rules framework. In the archived 1.2 description, its author specified Struggler or Wyrm attacks, Wyrm placement and occupation, and Wyrm or Unsimple fortification, alongside behavior controls, adaptation to player count and strength, bug control, smarter Australia/Asia and South America/North America play, and an improved "Floating Bubble" feature for concentrating armies on one territory. Its Cooperative flag defaults to False and controls only selected attack branches.
  • Frank+ (frank+.trp) modifies Frank with Vexer's player-elimination code, as credited by its author. This is documented code reuse, not just strategy inspiration. It targets vulnerable human players and helps finish winning games. Like Frank, its Cooperative flag defaults to False; it does not create an alliance with Frank.
  • Grudge (grudge.trp) tracks who captures its former territories and favors attacking the opponent responsible for the most losses. Human players win ties. If the target is unavailable, it attacks other opponents only at a force ratio of at least 1.5. This bot uses board snapshots rather than the action-history API, so captures measure hostile actions.
  • Hunter (hunter.trp) chooses an opponent and directs its play toward destroying that player, like pursuing a secret mission to eliminate a specific color. It also has mild same-program attack favoritism.
  • Kingmaker (kingmaker.trp) scores opponents by military and strategic strength, then directs attacks toward the leader.
  • Khorne (Khorne.trp) is an intentionally more aggressive code adaptation of Rambo.
  • Napoleon (napoleon.trp) tries to conquer and defend continents, and is described as best suited to games with two or three other computer players. Its author called it their first attempt to include continents in a strategy, stronger than Rambo, Simple, and Piranha, but still weak against experienced human players.
  • OddsMaker (oddsmaker.trp) estimates attack quality from force ratios and expected exchanges, avoids low-odds attacks, and concentrates armies at its best attack front.
  • Opportunist (opportunist.trp) re-evaluates attack opportunities using force ratios, eliminations, continent bonuses, and strategic access.
  • Piranha (piranha.trp) is an aggressive player that particularly targets human opponents. Its author described it as simple and especially hostile to human players.
  • Pitbull (pitbull.trp) focuses on retaking territories it has lost, continuing its attack during that turn until it succeeds or runs out of armies. Its author characterized it as a weak but focused opponent. It has mild same-program attack favoritism.
  • Quartermaster (quartermaster.trp) builds a concentrated striking force at a favorable front, moves its largest interior reserve toward the front, and avoids spreading placements evenly.
  • Rambo (rambo.trp) is a very aggressive, intentionally simple TurboRisk program. Its author described it as a much more aggressive program based on Simple.
  • Randy (randy.trp) uses substantial randomness in placement and target selection, with a preference for favorable force ratios and an early conquest. Its author said it was made to test computer opponents and hoped it could also stand up to human opponents.
  • Raptorbot (raptorbot.trp) is an expanded Simple-based player described as best suited to chaotic nine- or ten-player games. Its author said it grew from repeatedly modifying Simple and was their first attempt at a player; the author was still learning and expected to update it.
  • Shadow (shadow.trp) uses Doomstack's duel methods only in games that start with exactly two players. It concentrates on a strong front, keeps its main stack attacking after a conquest, plans affordable elimination routes, and consolidates through owned paths. All five routines switch to duel mode. Games that start with three or more players retain the Shadow method while any Vexer survives, even when only two players remain. In this mode it concentrates an army and follows Vexer across the map, without controlling turn order. It recognizes vexer.trp case-insensitively and uses public elimination history to wait until that Vexer has killed a player. It then attempts an elimination only if its stack can afford an estimated continuous route through all of that Vexer's remaining territories. The estimate budgets roughly 2.2 armies per defender, garrisons and a reserve, and is recomputed after each exchange; it is not a guarantee of victory. It also seeks one conquest per turn for a card, choosing the highest adjacent available-army ratio of at least 2.2, including Vexer before the trigger. After a conquest it stops ordinary attacks; affordable triggered eliminations remain allowed. It follows using adjacent friendly fortification. Multiple Vexers have independent triggers; after the last Vexer is eliminated by anyone, it switches immediately to Actuary's odds-based attacks, elimination planning, continent defense and reserve consolidation. Starting two-player games still use Doomstack. With no Vexer in the starting roster, it retains low-risk ordinary conquests. Shadow is an experimental repository bot created with AI assistance, not a proven counter to Vexer.
  • Shadow2 (shadow2.trp) is a replacement candidate based on Actuary, not the retired Shadow extension. It grows, takes cards, defends continents and considers profitable eliminations of either opponent without waiting for Vexer's first kill. It targets progressive card values with immediate captured-card trading. Elimination rewards conservatively count additional guaranteed sets at its own next trade value, subtracting sets already possible in its hand; unknown card types and later trade increases can make this an underestimate. Full continuous routes count losses against intervening opponents too, cannot cross owned or already captured territories, and charge garrisons and failure risk without an attrition discount. Planned kills need at least 60% estimated success and budget a five-army multiplayer reserve; captured cards cannot pay for their own capture. Single-territory kills use the same economics, while ordinary conquest benefits still count. Winning against the final opponent does not require card repayment or an extra reserve. Already-positive attack and elimination scores receive a 5% preference against exact, case-insensitive vexer.trp only with at least 75% estimated success. Staging mildly favors proximity to Vexer, worth at most one army on an owned front; equal strongest fronts favor the nearer one. Staging and these preferences are unchanged from the preceding Actuary-based candidate; no special border-defense priority is added. Elimination economics apply without Vexer too. Starting duels retain the Actuary foundation, not Doomstack. Route, odds, loss and reserve estimates are heuristics, not safety guarantees. Original Shadow and Actuary remain unchanged. Previous frozen Shadow2 experiments, including the preceding Actuary-based candidate, do not describe this unproven revision.
  • Shy (shy.trp) slowly expands, fortifies its frontiers, and tries to complete continents. It tends to start in South America.
  • Shy2 (shy2.trp) uses Shy's general approach without a fixed preference for South America.
  • Shy3 (shy3.trp) follows Shy's strategy but tends to start in Australia.
  • Simple (simple.trp) is a deliberately weak example demonstrating how to code a TurboRisk computer player; its author offered it as a starting point for creating other players.
  • Slave (slave.trp) chooses a "master" it will not attack, leaves the master's borders weakly defended, and attacks the master's enemies. If the master is eliminated, it switches to Digger's style. Its author described it as an experiment in this behavior, not a strategy intended to win. It also has mild same-program attack favoritism, separate from its master selection.
  • Struggler (struggler.trp) is an early attempt at a strong TurboRisk program; its author called it their first serious try.
  • Struggler+ (struggler+.trp) adds weak-human elimination and an improved end game to Struggler.
  • Tectonic (Tectonic.trp) is a code hybrid combining routines from Plates and Basic, rather than just strategy inspiration.
  • Turtle (turtle.trp) favors survival and a small, defensible frontier. It reinforces pressured positions and attacks only with a decisive advantage or for a low-risk card.
  • Vexer (vexer.trp) seeks weak-player eliminations under progression-based card rules. Under combination-based rules, its source describes it as a menace that eliminates weak players and attacks continents to deny bonuses.
  • Walker2 (Walker2.trp) is a simple cooperative program intended for testing other computer players; its source describes it as a weak opponent. It recognizes copies by script filename, not display name; see cooperation and program identity.
  • Walker3 (Walker3.trp) is a repository successor derived from Rafal Smotrzyk's Walker2. It cooperates only with other Walker3 copies, not Walker2. It adds compact assignment, continent-aware front reinforcement, a 2.2 available-army attack threshold, defensive occupation reserves, and adjacent owned-path fortification toward outsider fronts. It retains Walker2's territory-23 coalition endgame attack convention; see cooperation and program identity.
  • Washington (washington.trp) uses a Fabian-inspired approach: preserves forces, avoids unnecessary battles, reinforces exposed fronts, and advances from favorable positions.
  • Wyrm (wyrm.trp) is described by its author as a disorganized but cunning and ruthless opponent. The archived page says its author found it especially effective against many players and was still working on it; the current source describes it as best suited to four- or five-player games.
  • Zotob (zotob.trp) is a Wyrm/Descartes hybrid that changes strategy according to player count and its board position.
